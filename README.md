# Novelty ID vs Fake ID Under UK Law: Where the Line Actually Sits

Under UK law, the difference between a novelty ID and a fake ID comes down to three things: what the card imitates, what it was made to do, and how someone uses it. The main statutes in this area don't recognise “novelty ID” as a category at all. They talk about identity documents, false instruments and false representations, and a card either lands in one of those boxes or it doesn't.

That is good news for anyone who wants a card for a costume, a film set or a birthday gag. It is also a warning for anyone who thinks the word “novelty” on a checkout page settles the matter, because it doesn't. Below, we explain what the law actually looks at, how a lawful novelty card can slip into fake ID territory, and how venues and sellers deal with both. This is general information rather than legal advice, and it focuses on England and Wales.

## The difference at a glance

| **Question** | **Novelty ID** | **Fake ID** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What is it made for? | Fun: costumes, props, gifts, collecting | To be accepted as real by someone |
| Does it copy a real document? | No, it is an original design with no official name, crest or layout | Yes, or it imitates a type of document such as a driving licence |
| Does it claim an official source? | No | Yes, in effect |
| How is it used? | Worn, filmed, displayed or given as a gift | Shown to staff, shops or officials as genuine |
| Main laws in play | Usually none, unless it is misused | Identity Documents Act 2010, Forgery and Counterfeiting Act 1981, Fraud Act 2006 |
| Legal position | Generally lawful | Making, possessing or using it can be a criminal offence |

## The law has no box labelled “novelty”

Instead of asking whether a card is a novelty, the courts and prosecutors ask three questions.

### Does the card purport to be a specific official document?

The Identity Documents Act 2010 only applies to an “identity document”, which [section 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/40/body) defines as a document that is, or purports to be, an immigration document, a UK or foreign passport, a document that can be used instead of a passport, or a UK or foreign driving licence. Having a false one with an improper intention carries up to 10 years on indictment (section 4). Having one without a reasonable excuse carries up to 2 years on indictment (section 6).

A card made to look like a UK driving licence purports to be one. A bright, original design that claims to be nothing official doesn't.

There is also a trap here for cards styled as a “UK ID card”. Britain has no national ID card, so such a card imitates a document that doesn't exist. Licensing-trade press has long listed the so-called “UK national identification card”, made to look like a driving licence, alongside invented international driving permits and provisional motorcycle permits as typical fake ID, noting that [none of those documents exist](https://www.morningadvertiser.co.uk/Article/2012/08/22/Get-up-to-speed-with-false-ID-at-your-pub) (Morning Advertiser, 22 August 2012). Our guide to the [UK ID card](https://fakeiduk.co.uk/guides/uk-id-card/) covers what people are really searching for.

### Does the card lie about where it came from?

The Forgery and Counterfeiting Act 1981 defines a “false” instrument in [section 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/45/section/9) by reference to who appears to have made or authorised it, for example one that purports to have been made by a person who did not make it, or on the authority of someone who did not authorise it. A standard criminal law textbook ([Card, Cross and Jones, 2016](https://learninglink.oup.com/protected/files/content/file/1737466435824-cardmalloy22e_chapter_forgery_and_related_offences.pdf)) makes the point that an instrument is not false merely because it tells a lie.

Read plainly, a novelty card with its own branding claims no official source, while a card that borrows a government name or layout does. Courts decide how this applies to a particular card, so treat that as a guide, not a guarantee.

### Did anyone use it to deceive?

This is where most real cases land. [Section 2 of the Fraud Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/35/section/2/data.htm?view=plain) covers dishonestly making a false representation while intending to make a gain or cause loss. The Crown Prosecution Service [notes](https://cps.gov.uk/node/5731) that nobody has to actually gain or lose anything, and that the maximum sentence is 10 years. Forgery under [section 1 of the 1981 Act](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/45/section/1) turns on the intention that someone will accept the document as genuine and act on it.

## How a novelty card turns into a fake ID

The card itself rarely changes. These are the points where the label stops mattering:

- **It copies official design.** Government names, crests, layouts and driving licence or passport styling push a card toward the Identity Documents Act.
- **It imitates security features.** The PASS hologram is a registered trademark, and [PASS says](https://pass-scheme.org.uk/about-us) manufacturing cards with a similar mark is a criminal offence.
- **It is made or sold to pass checks.** Section 7 of the Fraud Act makes it an offence to [make, adapt, supply or offer to supply](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/35/notes/division/5/7) an article knowing it is designed or adapted for use in fraud, or intending it to be used that way. Home Office [guidance on the Act](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-fraud-act-2006-repeal-of-the-deception-offences-in-the-theft-acts-1968-1996) puts the penalty at up to 10 years. Listings that promise “scannable” cards or cards that “pass any check” describe exactly that purpose.
- **It is presented as real.** Handing a novelty card to a doorman or a shop assistant as proof of age, or to claim a student discount, is the Fraud Act offence in action.
- **It is used by an under-18 to buy alcohol.** [Section 149 of the Licensing Act 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/17/section/149) makes buying or attempting to buy alcohol an offence for an individual under 18 in England and Wales, with any ID.

## Does a “novelty only” disclaimer protect anyone?

Not on its own. A [Solicitors Journal article from 9 September 2008](https://mondaq.com/uk/privacy-protection/67356/who-goes-there) on novelty document websites pointed out that criminal liability can't be excluded by agreement, so terms and conditions don't work as a waiver, and that a “novelty purposes only” defence depends on whether a jury sees any other legitimate purpose. That commentary predates the current Identity Documents Act, so read it as a snapshot of the reasoning rather than a statement of today's law.

The Home Office's [False ID guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/false-id-guidance) (published 30 July 2012) goes further and notes that counterfeit ID cards are sold as novelty items, often by companies based abroad. In other words, the enforcement side already knows that “novelty” can be a label sellers use for cards that are anything but. A disclaimer states intent. Design and conduct decide the outcome.

## What venues do with both

The practical difference at the door is smaller than people expect. [Suffolk Trading Standards](https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/community-and-safety/suffolk-trading-standards/fair-trading/age-restricted-products) supports three main types of proof of age: photo driving licences, passports and PASS cards bearing the hologram. A novelty card is none of those, so it buys you nothing, and offering it as ID is the step that creates the legal problem.

## Four quick examples

- **A pink card with an invented layout and your photo, given as a gag birthday present.** Novelty. No real document is imitated and nobody is deceived.
- **A prop card for a film shoot with a fictional agency name.** Novelty. The purpose is entertainment.
- **A card laid out like a photocard driving licence and advertised as scannable.** Fake ID, and the seller is exposed as well as the buyer.
- **A novelty card handed to a doorman.** The card hasn't changed, but the conduct now looks like an attempt to deceive. That is Fraud Act territory.

## FAQ

### Is owning a fake ID always a crime?

Not automatically. Possession offences under the Identity Documents Act depend on whether the card is an “identity document” as the Act defines it, and on your intention or whether you have a reasonable excuse. Making a card with the intention that someone will accept it as genuine is forgery. Because the details matter, speak to a solicitor if you are in this situation.

### Can the same card be a novelty ID one day and a fake ID the next?

Yes. The card stays the same, but the law looks at use. A prop on a film set is a prop. The same card shown to staff as proof of age is evidence of an attempt to deceive.

### Which one carries heavier penalties?

The penalties attach to the offence, not the label. The Identity Documents Act (section 4), the Fraud Act (section 2) and the Forgery and Counterfeiting Act (section 1) each carry a maximum of 10 years on indictment, and section 6 of the Identity Documents Act carries up to 2 years on indictment. Those are ceilings, not typical sentences.

### Is the answer different in Scotland or Northern Ireland?

Somewhat. The Identity Documents Act applies across the UK, and the Fraud Act and Part 1 of the Forgery and Counterfeiting Act are marked as covering England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Scotland has its own legal system, and the Licensing Act 2003 offence above applies in England and Wales only, so check local sources if you are elsewhere.
